Dynamic Rail Analysis

In addition to static analysis, Voltus offers you the option of running dynamic gate-level analysis. You can use the dynamic approach to optimize the power network, minimize IR drop transients, and reduce leakage associated with added decoupling capacitance. Specifically, dynamic power-rail analysis can:

  • Detect power integrity problems.
  • Provide insight into the effect of local simultaneously switching high-power drivers.
  • Quantify instantaneous IR drop.
  • Assess the impact of resistance, capacitance, and inductance due to packaging, bond wire, and C4 bumps on transient IR drop.

Rather than averaging currents as static analysis does, dynamic analysis uses peak currents as the basis for the analysis, enabling you to see the finer time variation of currents over a clock cycle. Voltus's dynamic, or transient, approach to power-rail analysis uses a dynamic power or current waveform that you can generate either by performing logic simulation or by using timing window information. The analysis also includes the impact of package induance.

Power Integrity

Power is consumed in three basic ways in integrated circuits:

  • Switching power, which is the power consumed in the charging and discharging of interconnect capacitances. In most cases, this type of power consumption dominates because of large drivers having to drive large capacitive loads.
  • Internal power, which is the power consumed in the charging and discharging of the internal capacitances within gates and the capacitances in driving the load. It also includes feedthrough current from VDD to VSS, which occurs for a short time when both NMOS and PMOS devices are on. Feedthrough current is also known as crossbar, crowbar, or short-circuit current. The faster the slew rate, the less feedthrough current there is.
  • Leakage power, which is the power consumed by devices when they are not switching. It includes state-dependent leakage, which is leakage that depends on the state of the gate, that is, whether a transistor is on or off.

Source of Decoupling Capacitance

There are five kinds of decoupling capacitance that can impact dynamic power rail analysis:

  • Parasitic capacitance between power and ground
  • Device decoupling (diffusion and gate capacitance)
  • Discrete decoupling, added to the power rails
  • Natural decoupling capacitance from signal net capacitances through gates
  • Well capacitance

The well capacitance component is not currently modeled by Voltus, because the impact of this component is less than 10 percent for common process and design styles, when compared to the other four components. All other components of decoupling capacitance are accurately extracted using the embedded three-dimensional parasitic extraction engine, either during the processing of the main design input files or when generating the power grid views. A design can include all five forms. Decoupling capacitors operate as charge reservoirs to stabilize the power and ground grid and to minimize transients. Before the advent of 90-nanometer designs, design teams would typically add a significant number of decoupling capacitors between the power and ground rails to help smooth any voltage transients on the rails and to minimize transient voltage spikes caused by the switching of large buffers. As a result, the noise on the power grid was minimized.For 90-nanometer designs and below, however, the increased leakage from decoupling capacitors begins to add to the power consumption, so you can no longer liberally add them to minimize transients. For low-power and 90-nanometer designs, analyzing where to add and remove decoupling capacitors is of critical importance. Dynamic power-rail analysis gives you the necessary information to understand how to maximize the efficiency of the added decoupling capacitors to minimize the power consumption. Dynamic power-rail analysis helps you understand the optimal size of local decoupling capacitance, the optimal location in which to place the decoupling capacitors, and the impact of inductance on power rail transients. Adding decoupling capacitance is performed by replacing filler cells with filler cells containing decoupling capacitance (decaps). Filler cells are cells that do not have signal pins and do not consume any power. Decoupling capacitance cells are cells that do not have signal pins, but have an internal capacitance (mostly in the form of MOS gate capacitance) implemented. Because of the internal capacitance, they cause leakage current.

Simultaneous Switching

Groups of high-power drivers located in the same power region that switch synchronously drain power from the power rail and cause local transient IR drop in places, which must be included in the timing flow. Static analysis provides only limited insight into this problem. When your design has several simultaneously switching events and little decoupling capacitance, dynamic analysis enables you to identify optimal placement of decoupling capacitors to minimize transients. Instantaneous IR Drop

IR drop is an instantaneous phenomenon that is time-dependent and therefore only measurable by dynamic transient techniques. In addition, obtaining just the measurement of the IR drop is often insufficient. It is the shape and the magnitude of the IR drop that has the highest impact on timing. A large, very fast spike is typically recoverable and may have minimal impact on timing, but a spike of lesser magnitude but with a much longer duration can disrupt timing much more.Static analysis only gives a qualitative assessment of the design while dynamic analysis gives quantitative assessment. Static analysis give visibility into the quality of the grid, but not in absolute numbers over time as is done in dynamic analysis.

Packaging Parasitics

For designs 130 nanometers and smaller, parasitic resistance, capacitance, and inductance due to packaging, bond wire, and C4 bumps can have a significant impact on transient IR drop at modest frequencies. At higher frequencies, the resonance can interfere with the on-chip clocks. Inductance in particular becomes a problem outside the chip because of the effects of the bond wire and the packaging routing.Voltus accepts package RLC modeling in the form of SPICE subcircuits generated from different packaging modeling products such as Allegro. You can specify the name of a file containing a SPICE subcircuit describing a package model.
